The Great Emu War Of Nineteen Thirty Two

The horizon did not shimmer with heat alone. It vibrated. It was a low, rhythmic drumming that seemed to rise from the very red soil of the Western Australian outback, a sound like ten thousand distant hammers striking a hollow earth. Major G.P.W. Meredith of the Royal Australian Artillery stood on the edge of a golden wheat field in the Campion district, his eyes squinted against the brutal glare of the November sun. He was a veteran of the Great War, a man who had seen the mud of the Somme and the mechanical fury of the Western Front. He knew the sound of an approaching army. But this army did not wear boots. It did not carry rifles. It did not have a flag. It was composed of twenty thousand flightless birds, each standing six feet tall, with legs like pistons and eyes that flickered with a strange, prehistoric intelligence.

This was not a drill. This was not a joke. This was the opening movement of the most absurd military campaign in human history. The Australian military was officially at war with the emu.

To understand the madness, you must understand the desperation of the men who stood in those fields. These were the soldier-settlers... men who had survived the horrors of the trenches in Europe only to be given a patch of harsh, unforgiving land by a grateful government. They were told to grow wheat. They were promised a life of peace and prosperity. But the Great Depression had arrived like a cold wind, and the price of wheat had plummeted. And then came the birds.

The emu is a magnificent creature, a relic of an older world. For millennia, they had migrated from the coast to the interior, following the rains. But the humans had changed the map. They had cleared the scrub, dug deep wells, and planted thousands of acres of lush, green wheat. To the emus, the farms of Campion were not an intrusion... they were an invitation. It was the ultimate all-you-can-eat buffet. Twenty thousand birds descended upon the region, trampling fences with their powerful three-toed feet and devouring the crops that represented the last hope of the struggling veterans.

The farmers were desperate. They had tried fences. They had tried shotguns. But the emus were too many, too fast, and too resilient. In a move of pure, bureaucratic desperation, a delegation of veterans traveled to Perth to meet with the Minister of Defence, Sir George Pearce. They didn't ask for money. They didn't ask for subsidies. They asked for machine guns. They wanted a military solution to a biological problem.

Pearce, perhaps seeing an opportunity for a bit of easy propaganda and target practice for his troops, agreed. He assigned Major Meredith, two soldiers—Sergeant S. McMurray and Gunner J. O'Halloran—and two Lewis automatic machine guns to the task. They also carried ten thousand rounds of ammunition. The soldiers arrived in Campion with a sense of grim confidence. They were professionals. They had fought the Kaiser’s army. Surely, they thought, a few oversized chickens would be no match for the pinnacle of British engineering.

But as the first skirmish began, the soldiers realized they had made a grave tactical error. They had underestimated the enemy.

The date was November second, nineteen thirty two. A large flock of about fifty emus was spotted near the town of Campion. Meredith ordered his men to move into position. The Lewis gun was set up on its bipod, its barrel gleaming in the sun. The soldiers waited until the birds were within range. The command was given. Fire!

The Lewis gun rattled, a rapid-fire staccato that echoed across the plains. Dust kicked up in small fountains. But as the smoke cleared, the soldiers looked on in disbelief. The emus had not panicked. They had not huddled together. At the first sound of gunfire, the flock had split into small, disorganized groups. They ran in every direction, their long necks bobbing, their legs churning the dust. They were moving at nearly thirty miles per hour, zigzagging with a grace that made them almost impossible to lead with a heavy weapon. By the time the magazine was empty, only a handful of birds lay in the dirt. The rest had vanished into the scrub, leaving the soldiers staring at the empty horizon.

Major Meredith was perplexed. He moved his unit further south, where a flock of over a thousand birds had been reported near a local dam. This, he thought, would be the decisive blow. He set up an ambush near the water’s edge, concealing the guns behind thick bushes. He waited in the stifling heat, the flies crawling over his face, until the massive herd arrived to drink. It was a sea of feathers... a target so large and so dense that even a blind man could not miss.

They waited until the birds were just a few hundred yards away. Meredith held his breath. Now!

The gun fired a few rounds... and then it jammed.

The metallic click of the failure sounded like a thunderclap in the sudden silence. The emus, alerted by the noise, didn't wait for a second chance. They erupted into a frenzy of motion. Before O’Halloran could clear the breech, the birds were gone. It was as if they possessed a collective sixth sense, a tactical intuition that allowed them to exploit the mechanical weaknesses of their attackers.

As the days turned into weeks, the reports from the front grew increasingly surreal. The emus, it seemed, had developed a sophisticated command structure. Major Meredith noted with a mix of frustration and grudging respect that each flock appeared to have a "scout"—a large, dark-plumaged male that would stand perfectly still on a high mound while the rest of the birds ate. This sentinel would keep a keen eye on the horizon. At the first sign of a khaki uniform or the glint of a barrel, he would give a low, guttural warning cry. The flock would instantly disperse, moving with a calculated chaos that baffled the military mind.

The Major’s frustration turned to obsession. He decided that the problem was mobility. If the guns were too slow on the ground, they would take them to the road. He ordered a local farmer to bring out a heavy truck. One of the Lewis guns was mounted on the back of the vehicle, pointing toward the rear. The plan was to chase the birds across the open plains, firing at them while moving at high speed.

It was a disaster. The Australian outback is not a paved highway. It is a labyrinth of ruts, boulders, and hidden rabbit holes. As the truck roared across the terrain, bouncing violently, the gunner found it impossible to even hold the weapon, let alone aim it. The emus, meanwhile, simply outpaced the vehicle, darting through thickets where the truck could not follow. The chase ended when the truck hit a particularly deep gully, nearly tossing the soldiers into the dust and snapping the mounting of the gun. The emus, observing from a safe distance, seemed almost to be mocking them.

By the end of the first week, the military had expended nearly three thousand rounds of ammunition. The official "body count" was estimated to be only a few hundred birds. In the parliament in Canberra, the news was met with ridicule. Opposition members asked if a medal was being struck for the emus. One politician famously asked if the military intended to declare war on the kangaroos next.

Sir George Pearce, the Minister of Defence, was humiliated. He ordered the withdrawal of the troops. Major Meredith returned to the city, but his report on the campaign was a masterpiece of military justification. He compared the emus to the finest guerrilla fighters in history. He wrote... if we had a military division with the bullet-carrying capacity of these birds, it would face any army in the world. They can face machine guns with the invulnerability of tanks.

But the farmers were not laughing. The withdrawal of the military meant the return of the birds. The destruction of the wheat continued unabated. Under intense political pressure, the government ordered a second campaign a few weeks later. Meredith returned to the field, determined to redeem his reputation. This time, he was more systematic. He used the local farmers as beaters, driving the birds toward the guns. The kill count rose. On some days, they claimed to have downed three hundred birds.

But the math was still against them. There were twenty thousand emus. Even after a month of sustained "warfare," the impact on the total population was negligible. The emus were breeding faster than the Lewis guns could fire. The soldiers were exhausted, the equipment was breaking down, and the cost of the ammunition was becoming a scandal. In December of nineteen thirty two, the Great Emu War officially came to an end. The military retreated for the final time.

The birds had won.

In the years that followed, the government realized that machine guns were not the answer to an ecological crisis. They eventually moved to a much more effective, if less dramatic, solution... a bounty system. They offered a small payment for every emu head brought in by the farmers themselves. In a single year, over fifty thousand bounties were claimed. The people of the outback, it turned out, were much better at protecting their own land than a formal military unit with outdated tactics.

The Great Emu War remains one of the most bizarre footnotes in the history of the twentieth century. It is a story of human arrogance meeting the immovable force of nature. It is a reminder that technology, no matter how advanced, is often powerless against the simple, ancient instincts of survival. The emus didn't need a strategy room. They didn't need a supply line. They only needed their legs, their speed, and the vastness of the Australian scrub.

Today, if you travel through the wheat belt of Western Australia, you will still see the emus. They stand in the shadows of the trees, watching the cars go by with that same unblinking, prehistoric gaze. They are the descendants of the veterans of nineteen thirty two... the birds that stood their ground against the Royal Australian Artillery and walked away.

The farmers still grow wheat. The sun still bakes the red earth. And the legend of the war continues to be told in the pubs and around the campfires of the outback. It is told with a shake of the head and a bit of a laugh... a story of the time when the might of the British Empire was humbled by a flock of flightless birds who simply refused to die.

Major Meredith went on to other assignments, his name forever linked to the campaign. Sir George Pearce’s political career continued, though he was forever haunted by the nickname "The Minister of the Emu War." And the Lewis guns were eventually retired, replaced by weapons that were never again asked to fire upon the wildlife of the continent.

As the twilight falls over the Campion district, and the golden wheat ripples in the evening breeze, you can almost see them. A long line of dark shapes moving across the horizon. They move with a quiet, confident grace, their bobbing heads silhouetted against the purple sky. They are the masters of the plains. They are the victors of a war that should never have happened. And as they vanish into the gathering dark, the only sound is the wind in the grass... a soft, whispering reminder that in the battle between man and the wild, the wild always has the last word.

The war is over. The silence has returned. But the memory of the twenty thousand remains... a ghost army of feathers and dust that proved, once and for all, that some things are simply too wild to be conquered.
