The Night Witches

The engine of the Polikarpov biplane did not roar... it rattled like a bucket of loose bolts. It was a sound that suggested the aircraft was held together by nothing more than prayer and piano wire. Polina sat in the front cockpit, her fingers numb inside her oversized leather gloves. The wind whipped through the open canopy, biting at the small patch of exposed skin between her goggles and her fleece-lined helmet. She looked over the side, but there was nothing to see. The world below was a vast, suffocating inkwell.

Behind her, in the second cockpit, Raisa was huddled over a map. She was the navigator, the eyes of their tiny, wooden bird. Raisa tapped Polina on the shoulder and pointed toward the horizon.

Five minutes, Polina! Raisa shouted over the wind. Keep your eyes on the stars. If we lose the North Star, we are ghosts.

Polina nodded, though she knew Raisa couldn't see it. They were part of the Five-Hundred and Eighty-Eighth Night Bomber Regiment. To the Soviet command, they were a daring experiment. To the German soldiers huddled in the trenches below, they were something else entirely. They were the Nachthexen. The Night Witches.

Their planes were relics—Po-2 biplanes designed for dusting crops, not for fighting the most advanced military machine on earth. They were made of plywood and canvas. They had no radios. They had no parachutes. They had no guns for defense. They carried only two things... a pair of heavy bombs strapped to the wings and the cold, sharp will to strike back at the invaders who had burned their villages and stolen their brothers.

Polina adjusted the throttle. She could feel the vibration of the wooden frame beneath her. It felt fragile, like the skeleton of a bird.

We are crossing the line, Raisa called out.

The darkness below changed. Tiny, pinprick flickers of light appeared—the glowing embers of German campfires. Further ahead, the shadows became more defined. The target was a supply depot at a railway junction. It was a vital artery for the German push toward the east, guarded by a ring of heavy anti-aircraft guns and the most terrifying weapon of all... the searchlights.

Quiet now, Polina whispered to herself.

She reached for the ignition switch. With a sharp click, she cut the engine.

The rattling stopped instantly. The silence that followed was heavy and absolute. The biplane did not fall—it glided. The only sound now was the eerie, high-pitched whistling of the wind passing through the wire stays between the wings. To the Germans on the ground, this sound was the only warning they ever received. It sounded like a broomstick cutting through the air.

Polina felt the plane dip as the air pressure shifted. They were a shadow within a shadow. They were invisible to radar because they were made of wood. They were invisible to the ear because their engines were dead.

Left... five degrees left, Raisa whispered.

Polina banked the plane. The canvas wings groaned under the strain. She could see the outlines of the fuel trucks now, parked in neat rows like sleeping beetles. She waited, her heart thumping a frantic rhythm against her ribs. She was nineteen years old. Six months ago, she had been a student of mathematics in Moscow. Now, she was a ghost in a plywood kite.

Now! Raisa screamed.

Polina pulled the manual release lever. Two heavy thuds vibrated through the airframe as the bombs were uncoupled. The plane surged upward, suddenly lightened of its burden.

Below, the world exploded.

A pillar of orange fire erupted from the depot, lighting up the clouds from beneath. The shockwave reached them seconds later, buffeting the tiny plane like a leaf in a storm. The silence was over.

The German searchlights snapped on.

Long, blue-white fingers of light began to claw at the sky, searching for the source of the destruction. One beam swept past them, then another. Polina dove, trying to stay in the darkness, but it was too late. A searchlight caught their tail.

The world turned blindingly white. Polina squinted, her vision swimming.

They have us! Polina shouted.

The anti-aircraft guns began to bark—the rhythmic, terrifying thump-thump-thump of flak. Shrapnel whistled past the wings, punching jagged holes in the canvas. A Po-2 did not need much to catch fire. A single tracer bullet could turn their plane into a falling torch.

Polina kicked the rudder, zig-zagging through the sky. She felt a sharp tug on the stick as a piece of metal sliced through a control wire. The plane became sluggish, pulling hard to the right.

Raisa! I cannot hold the level!

I see it! Raisa yelled. She stood up in her cockpit, leaning out over the edge into the freezing gale. She grabbed the loose end of the wire, her bare hands bleeding as she pulled it tight against the frame. Hold it, Polina! I have the tension!

It was madness. They were three thousand feet in the air, flying a broken biplane through a forest of fire, held together by a girl’s grip.

Polina slammed the ignition. The engine coughed, sputtered, and then roared back to life with a desperate, metallic shriek. She pushed the nose down, diving toward the safety of the dark forest beyond the tracks.

The searchlight followed them for a few more agonizing seconds, then lost them in the smoke of the burning depot. The flak faded into a distant rumble.

They were in the dark again.

Raisa slumped back into her seat. Polina could hear her labored breathing over the engine’s rattle. We are clear... we are clear.

They flew for another hour, the wind howling through the holes in their wings. The cold was a physical weight now, pressing into their bones. Every time Polina felt like she could no longer hold the stick, she thought of the letters her mother had sent... letters that spoke of hunger and the sound of boots on the street. She tightened her grip.

When the silhouettes of their hidden airfield appeared in the grey light of dawn, Polina felt a wave of exhaustion so profound she nearly fainted. She lined up the plane with the grass strip marked by two tiny lanterns. The landing was rough, the wooden wheels bouncing on the uneven ground, but the plane held.

As the propeller spun to a halt, the silence returned.

A group of women ran toward them—other pilots, mechanics, and armorers. They reached the plane and helped Polina and Raisa climb out. Raisa’s hands were frozen in the shape of the control wire, the blood having turned to black ice on her skin.

Did you hit it? a young mechanic asked, her eyes wide.

The depot is gone, Polina said. Her voice was cracked and thin.

They walked toward the mess tent, leaning on each other. Polina looked back at their plane. It was a mess of shredded canvas and scorched wood. It looked like a toy that had been chewed by a giant. She realized then that the Germans were right to be afraid. It wasn't the planes that were dangerous. It was the fact that there was nothing—not the cold, not the fire, not the fear of death—that could stop them from flying again the next night.

They were the Night Witches. And as long as the sky was dark, they would be the ones to carry the fire.

Polina sat down on a wooden bench and took a mug of hot, bitter tea. She looked at Raisa, who was finally smiling as a medic bandaged her hands. They didn't need medals. They didn't need recognition. They had the stars, the wind, and the knowledge that for one more night, the invaders had looked at the sky and seen only their own end.

The sun began to rise over the Eastern Front, a pale, cold orb that promised nothing but more war. But in the small, hidden camp of the Five-Hundred and Eighty-Eighth, there was a feeling of triumph that no winter could freeze.
