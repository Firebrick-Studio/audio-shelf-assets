The Conman Who Sold The World The Legend Of Natwarlal

The year is nineteen ninety six. New Delhi Railway Station is a chaotic symphony of steam, shouting vendors, and the relentless thrum of thousands of feet. Amidst the swirling crowds, a frail, elderly man sits in a wheelchair, flanked by three armed police officers. He looks broken... a ghost of a man at eighty four years old, being transported to a hospital for treatment. He is Mithilesh Kumar Srivastava, but the world knows him by a name that makes judges tremble and lawmen sigh in frustration... Natwarlal.

The officers turn their heads for a single, fleeting second to navigate through a surge of passengers. When they look back, the wheelchair is empty. The old man... the man who could barely walk... has vanished into the sea of humanity. He would never be seen again. This was the final vanishing act of India’s greatest artist of the con. A man who did not just steal money... he stole monuments.

To understand how a boy from a small village in Bihar became a man who could sell the Taj Mahal, we must look at the hands. Watch them closely. They are the hands of a master calligrapher. As a young student, Mithilesh realized he had a terrifying gift. He could look at any signature... any complex scroll of ink... and replicate it with such precision that even the original writer could not tell the difference. He discovered this power by accident when a neighbor asked him to help with some paperwork. Mithilesh realized then that in a world of bureaucracy, the person who controls the pen controls reality itself.

His first marks were small. A forged check here, a fake government permit there. But Mithilesh had a hunger that went beyond petty theft. He was a storyteller. He understood that people do not buy products... they buy the story you tell them. And more importantly, he understood the psychology of greed. He knew that if you offer someone a deal that is too good to be true, their brain will fight to believe it is real.

Imagine the hot, dusty afternoon in Agra. The Taj Mahal stands in the distance, a shimmering vision of white marble. A group of wealthy foreign tourists is approached by a man of impeccable stature. He wears the sharp, starched uniform of a high-ranking government official. He carries leather-bound folders embossed with the official seal of the Republic of India. He introduces himself with a calm, bureaucratic authority. He tells them a secret... a tragedy of economics. He explains that the government is in a desperate financial crisis and has made a quiet, heartbreaking decision. They are selling the Taj Mahal to a private investor to save the national treasury.

The tourists are stunned. It is impossible. It is madness. But then, Mithilesh opens the folder. He shows them the deeds. He shows them the official parliamentary orders, signed by the President, stamped with the Great Seal. He speaks of the need for absolute discretion. If the public found out, there would be a revolution. This is why the price is so low... a mere fraction of its worth. He leads them through the gardens, pointing out the maintenance costs and the structural integrity with the bored tone of a man who has seen these figures a thousand times.

They believe him. They do not just believe him... they are desperate to be the ones who "own" a wonder of the world. They sign the papers. They hand over the money in stacks of high-value currency. Mithilesh shakes their hands, thanks them for their service to the nation, and walks calmly toward a waiting car. By the time the tourists realize they have bought a pile of marble that no one can actually own, Mithilesh is three states away, already planning his next "sale."

He did not stop at the Taj Mahal. Over the course of his career, he "sold" the Red Fort in Delhi. He "sold" the massive, sprawling Rashtrapati Bhavan... the official residence of the President. He even managed to "sell" the Parliament House itself. Legend has it that the sale of the Parliament included all the sitting members of the house as "incidental property." He was a man who looked at the most sacred symbols of the state and saw them as inventory.

How did he get away with it? It was the theater of it all. Natwarlal would rent expensive cars, hire "staff" who believed they were working for the government, and use the sheer weight of Indian bureaucracy as a shield. He knew that no one questions a man who looks like he belongs in a position of power. He was a master of a hundred aliases and a thousand disguises. To some, he was a textile merchant. To others, a social worker. To the police, he was a ghost who left behind only the smell of expensive cologne and a trail of forged signatures.

But every great hunter is eventually cornered. Natwarlal was caught many times. He was sentenced to a total of over one hundred and thirteen years in prison across multiple states. If he had served his time, he would have lived to be two hundred. But the walls of a prison could not hold a man who could talk his way through a locked door.

In one instance, he was being held in a high-security cell. He called for the guard, complaining of a severe chest pain. As the guard leaned in to check on him, Natwarlal didn't strike him. He spoke to him. He whispered about a hidden cache of gold buried near the guard's home village. He gave specific details... landmarks that only a local would know. He told the guard that if he just "happened" to leave the cell door unlocked during the shift change, the gold would be his. The guard, blinded by the same greed that Natwarlal had exploited in his victims, turned the key. Natwarlal walked out the front gate in a borrowed uniform, tipping his cap to the warden on the way out.

He was a hero in his home village of Bangra. To the people there, he was a modern-day Robin Hood. They didn't see a criminal... they saw a man who took money from the greedy and the foreign and brought it back to the soil. He funded weddings, he paid for the education of the village children, and he ensured that no one in Bangra went hungry. When the police would come looking for him, the villagers would simply point toward the horizon and say they hadn't seen him in years, while Mithilesh sat in a nearby house, sipping tea and laughing at the absurdity of it all.

The escapes became more legendary than the crimes. Nine times he was captured. Nine times he vanished. He escaped from moving trains. He escaped from hospitals. He once escaped from a prison by simply convincing the guards that he was a senior inspector sent to test their security. By the time they realized their mistake, he was already miles away, likely planning his next heist.

As he aged, the myth of Natwarlal grew. People began to attribute almost supernatural powers to him. They said he could change his face at will. They said he could speak every language in the world. He became a folk hero, a symbol of the little man outsmarting the massive, slow-moving machine of the state.

Then came that final day in nineteen ninety six. The railway station escape was his masterpiece. At eighty four, most people are thinking of the end. Natwarlal was thinking of the next act. He knew the police would be relaxed, thinking his age had finally tethered him to the earth. He used their own assumptions against them. He waited for the noise, the distraction, the one moment of broken focus. And then... he was gone.

The authorities were embarrassed. They searched every hospital, every village, every hiding spot they knew. Nothing. Three years later, his brother claimed that Mithilesh had died in nineteen ninety six, shortly after the escape. But then, a lawyer claimed he had seen him alive and well in nineteen ninety nine. In two thousand and nine, his brother filed another petition, claiming Mithilesh had finally passed away at the age of ninety seven.

But did he? In the world of Natwarlal, the truth is whatever you are willing to believe. Even in death, he was performing a con. He left the world wondering if he was truly gone or if he was somewhere in a quiet village, watching the news and waiting for the right moment to sell the moon to an unsuspecting billionaire.

The story of Natwarlal is a reminder of the thin line between reality and perception. We believe in the value of money because we are told it has value. We believe in the ownership of monuments because we see a deed. He showed the world that all these things are just stories we tell each other. And if you are a better storyteller than the government, you can own anything you want.

Today, if you visit the Taj Mahal, you will see the crowds and the majesty of the marble. You might see a man in a sharp suit carrying a folder, looking at the building with a calculating eye. You might wonder, just for a second, if the deed in his hand is real. And if he approaches you with a smile and a secret, remember the name of Mithilesh Kumar Srivastava. Because the greatest trick the devil ever pulled was convincing the world he didn't exist... but the greatest trick Natwarlal ever pulled was selling the world a piece of itself.

He remains the patron saint of the audacious. A man who proved that with a pen, a smile, and a deep understanding of the human heart, you can turn the entire world into your marketplace. He didn't just sell the Taj Mahal... he sold the idea that anything is possible if you have the courage to ask for the check.

As the sun sets over the station where he was last seen, the echoes of the steam whistles seem to carry his laughter. A reminder that somewhere, in the shadows of the monuments he once "owned," the legend of the man who sold the world continues to grow, one signature at a time.
