The Siege Of The Sun

The shell-horn did not call for the morning... it called for the end. Cuetzpalli stood on the flat roof of the House of Arrows, his chest heaving, his skin slick with a cold sweat that had nothing to do with the morning mist of Lake Texcoco. Below him, the Venice of the West was bleeding. Tenochtitlan—the city of floating gardens, white stone, and emerald feathers—was becoming a labyrinth of smoke and silence. The Great Market of Tlatelolco, where once a hundred thousand people traded jade for cacao and leopard skins for gold, was now a scorched wasteland. The air did not smell of flowers or roasting maize anymore. It smelled of the great rash... and the heavy, metallic tang of blood that had baked into the stone under the relentless heat of the Mexican sun.

Cuetzpalli was a runner. In the years of the Sun’s glory, he had been the wind on the causeways, carrying fresh fish from the coast to the Emperor’s table in less than a day. His legs were cords of tempered muscle, and his heart was a drum that never tired. But today, he was not carrying fish. He was carrying the last hope of a dying world.

Tied to his waist was a small pouch of deer-hide. Inside was a message from the young Emperor, Cuauhtémoc—the Descending Eagle. It was not written in the colorful pictograms of the old codices. There was no time for art. It was a verbal command, memorized and repeated until it was a part of Cuetzpalli’s soul. He was to reach the hidden resistance in the northern districts... and tell them to wait for the signal of the fire-arrow.

Go, Runner, the Emperor had whispered in the darkness of the palace. Run until your lungs burst. If you fall, the Sun sets forever.

Cuetzpalli leaped from the roof. He landed on the edge of a canal, his sandals slapping against the wet stone. The water of the lake, once blue and teeming with life, was now a stagnant grey, choked with the debris of the siege. He didn't look back. He ran toward the Great Teocalli... the massive pyramid of Huitzilopochtli.

The city was a cage. The bearded ones—the men of metal—had cut the causeways. They had destroyed the aqueducts. For eighty days, the city had been thirsty. For eighty days, the people had eaten the bark of trees and the salt-grass of the lake. Cuetzpalli passed an old woman sitting by a fountain that had long since run dry. She didn't look at him. She was staring at her hands, which were covered in the weeping sores of the smallpox... the invisible enemy that had traveled on the breath of the Spaniards.

He reached the edge of the residential district. Suddenly, a sound like a thunderclap ripped through the air.

Boom.

It was the thunder-sticks of the Metal Men. A lead ball struck a stone wall inches from Cuetzpalli’s head, showering him in white dust. He dove into the shadows of a narrow alleyway. He could hear them now—the rhythmic clank of iron armor and the terrifying, rhythmic breathing of the "iron deer." The horses.

To the Aztecs, the horse was a nightmare made of flesh and thunder. They were monsters that didn't know how to die. Cuetzpalli pressed his back against the cool stone, holding his breath. He saw a shadow pass the end of the alley. A Spaniard... his face hidden behind a grilled helmet of steel, his sword dripping with a red that looked black in the dim light. Behind him came the Tlaxcalans—the old enemies of the Aztecs—who had joined the Metal Men to see the great city fall.

Cuetzpalli waited until the sound of the iron boots faded. He moved like a jaguar, silent and low to the ground. He climbed a wall, scrambling over a garden of wilted marigolds, and dropped into the next street.

He was halfway to Tlatelolco when he saw the "Mountain of the Sun." The Great Temple was a silhouette against the bruised purple of the morning sky. But something was wrong. The twin shrines at the top were not glowing with the sacred fires. Instead, they were wreathed in the black, oily smoke of a common blaze. The Spaniards were up there. They were casting down the statues of the gods, their laughter echoing over the city like the barking of dogs.

They do not know... Cuetzpalli whispered. They do not know that the gods are not the stone. The gods are the people.

He reached the main canal that separated the two halves of the city. Usually, a hundred canoes would be darting across the water, filled with flowers and fruit. Now, there was only the silence of the dead. He saw a Spanish brigantine—a wooden fortress on the water—patrolling the center of the lake. Its white sails looked like the wings of a predatory bird.

Cuetzpalli dove into the water. The lake was cold and tasted of salt and rot. He swam beneath the surface, his lungs screaming for air, until his fingers touched the wooden pilings of a floating garden. He pulled himself up, gasping, hidden by the tall, yellowed reeds.

From here, he could see the final stand.

The warriors of the Eagle and the Jaguar were gathered on the plazas of Tlatelolco. They were skeletal shadows of the men they once were, their obsidian-edged swords chipped and dull. But they stood with a defiance that the Metal Men could not understand. They were not fighting for land. They were fighting for the memory of their ancestors.

Cuetzpalli climbed out of the mud and began the final sprint. His legs were heavy now, the muscles cramping from the cold water and the lack of food. Every breath was a jagged piece of glass in his throat.

He saw a group of Tlaxcalan scouts blocking the way. They had seen him. They raised their spears and let out a war-cry.

Cuetzpalli didn't slow down. He couldn't. He was the message.

He dodged the first spear, the wood whistling past his ear. He used his momentum to shoulder-charge the second scout, sending him sprawling into the canal. He didn't stay to fight. He leaped over a pile of rubble and sprinted toward the temple of Tlatelolco.

A Spaniard on a white horse emerged from behind a ruined wall. The man lowered his lance, the steel tip glinting with a murderous light.

Cuetzpalli looked at the horse. He saw the foam at its mouth and the madness in its eyes. He didn't run away. He ran toward it. At the last possible second, he dropped to his knees and slid through the mud. The lance passed over him, and the horse jumped, its heavy hooves narrowly missing his head.

He was past them.

He reached the steps of the temple. A warrior in a tattered eagle-feather suit caught him as he stumbled.

Who are you? The warrior demanded, his hand on a stone knife.

The Descending Eagle... Cuetzpalli wheezed, clutching his deer-hide pouch. The signal... the fire-arrow. Tell the lords... tell them the Sun must be hidden.

The warrior’s eyes widened. He understood. This was the command to stop the open battle—to hide the gold, to hide the codices, to hide the children. The city was lost, but the people must survive.

Cuetzpalli collapsed onto the stone. He watched as the warrior ran up the steps, shouting the command. A few minutes later, a single arrow, tipped with a flaming rag, streaked across the dark sky. It was a beautiful, lonely arc of light.

Then, the final assault began.

The Spanish cannons roared. The walls of the temple shook as if the earth itself was having a seizure. Cuetzpalli lay there, watching the smoke rise. He saw the Spaniards breach the plaza. He saw the glint of the steel swords as they moved through the ranks of the Aztec warriors. It was a harvest of iron.

The Emperor was captured that afternoon. The Descending Eagle was led away in chains, his head held high even as the city crumbled around him.

Cuetzpalli was not captured. He was just another body among the thousands in the plaza. He crawled into the shadows of a fallen statue—a great stone serpent that had been cracked in two. He lay there as the night fell, listening to the sounds of the Spaniards celebrating. They were shouting for gold. They were tearing the city apart, looking for the treasures of Montezuma.

They will find the gold, Cuetzpalli thought, his eyes closing. But they will never find the heart.

He reached into his mind and began to recite the poems he had learned as a boy. He recited the names of the days. He recited the movements of the stars. He recited the story of how the world was created from the back of a giant crocodile. He was no longer a runner. He was a library.

A few hours later, the rain began to fall. It was a heavy, tropical downpour that washed the blood from the stones and hissed in the embers of the burning palaces. Cuetzpalli felt the water on his face. It tasted clean.

He dragged himself up. He was a ghost in a city of ghosts. He began to walk away from the center, moving toward the mountains that ringed the valley. He wasn't alone. In the darkness, he saw other shadows moving—mothers carrying children, old men clutching small bundles of seeds, warriors who had stripped off their feathers to blend into the night.

They were leaving Tenochtitlan. The city was dying, but the Aztecs were not. They were carrying the seeds of their culture into the high places, into the hidden valleys where the Metal Men could not follow.

Cuetzpalli stopped at the edge of the lake and looked back. The Great Temple was a jagged ruin against the stars. The "Siege of the Sun" was over. A new era had begun—an era of iron, and crosses, and a language he didn't know.

But as he touched the deer-hide pouch at his waist, he felt a flicker of heat. The message had been delivered. The people would remember. They would remember the floating gardens. They would remember the music of the shell-horns. They would remember that once, they lived in a city made of light and stone.

The Sun sets, he whispered to the wind. But the stars remain.

He turned toward the mountains and began to run. He didn't run with the speed of a messenger anymore. He ran with the steady, rhythmic pace of a man who has a very long journey ahead of him. He ran because as long as one person was moving, the story was still being written.

Tenochtitlan was gone. But the heart of the runner still beat... and in that heartbeat, the city lived on. The Metal Men had conquered the stone, but they had failed to conquer the horizon. And in the silence of the Mexican night, the only sound was the steady, persistent slap of sandals against the earth... the sound of a history that refused to be buried.
