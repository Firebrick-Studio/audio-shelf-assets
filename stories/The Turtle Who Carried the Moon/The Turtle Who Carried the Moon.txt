The Turtle Who Carried the Moon

Have you ever listened to the sound of the world when it is perfectly, completely still? If you listen very closely, past the rustle of your blankets and the soft breath of the wind outside your window, you might hear the rhythm of the Silver Sedge Marsh. It is a slow rhythm... like a giant, sleeping heart. Thrum... thrum... thrum. In the heart of this marsh lived a turtle named Barnaby. Barnaby was not fast. He was not sleek. His shell was the color of old moss and river stones, and his eyes were as golden as a sunset over the water. While the dragonflies zipped like tiny blue bolts of lightning and the frogs leaped in great, splashing arcs, Barnaby moved with a steady, quiet purpose. One step... then a long, thoughtful pause... and then another.

One Tuesday evening, the sky was a deep, velvet indigo. The stars were just beginning to wake up, blinking their sleepy eyes in the darkness. High above, the moon was a bright, shimmering crescent, looking like a silver boat sailing on a sea of shadows. But then, something strange happened. There was a sound... a tiny, crystalline tink... like a glass marble hitting a marble floor. A single, glowing sliver of the moon had broken off and began to tumble through the air. It fell through the clouds, spinning and glowing, until it landed with a soft hiss right in the middle of the Silver Sedge Marsh.

The animals gathered around the fallen light. It was a beautiful thing, curved like a feather and glowing with a cold, pale light that made the mud look like diamonds. But as they watched, the glow began to fade. The moon-sliver was turning a dull, dusty grey.

We must get it back, hooted the Owl, fluttering down from a willow tree. It belongs in the sky. If it stays on the ground, the night will lose its magic.

I can do it, chirped Finnegan the Rabbit, twitching his pink nose. I am the fastest creature in the woods. I will hop to the top of the High Peak and toss it back to its mother.

Finnegan scooped up the moon-sliver. It was light, but it was slippery, like a wet bar of soap. He began to run. He zipped past the reeds—zip, zip, zip—and bounced over the logs. But Finnegan was too fast. He hit a patch of damp moss and slid. The moon-sliver flew out of his paws and tumbled into the dirt.

It is too slippery, Finnegan panted, his chest heaving. It does not want to stay still.

Then the Fox tried. He was graceful and clever. He tried to carry the sliver in his mouth, but the cold light made his teeth ache, and he had to set it down. One by one, the animals tried to rush the moon-sliver back to the High Peak, and one by one, they failed. They were too jittery, or too impatient, or too distracted by the shadows.

Finally, Barnaby the turtle crawled forward. He looked at the dimming piece of the sky and then at the distant, dark mountain known as the High Peak.

I will take it, Barnaby said. His voice was like the sound of dry leaves rubbing together... slow and peaceful.

The other animals laughed. Barnaby, the peak is miles away, the Fox said. By the time you get there, the sun will be up, and the moon-sliver will have turned to common stone.

Barnaby did not argue. He simply lowered his head and allowed the Owl to place the silver sliver onto the very center of his flat, sturdy shell. It felt cold against his back... a deep, ancient cold that smelled of stardust and winter air.

Then, Barnaby began to walk.

Step... pause... breathe. Step... pause... breathe.

The first hour was the hardest. He had to cross the Great Mud Flat. Every time he moved a leg, the mud tried to pull him down with a wet, sucking sound. Schlopp... schlopp. To a rabbit, this would have been a nightmare. But Barnaby did not mind the mud. He knew that if he moved slowly enough, the mud would let him go. He kept his shell level. He kept the moon-sliver balanced. He watched the way the light from his back reflected in the tiny puddles, creating miniature galaxies at his feet.

As he walked, a micro-conflict appeared in the form of a Narrow Stream. The water was rushing quickly, bubbling over the rocks with a cheerful but dangerous song. A single slip would wash the moon-sliver away into the deep river. Barnaby reached the edge and stopped. He waited. He watched the rhythm of the water. He saw that every few seconds, the current slowed behind a large, grey rock.

He waited for the slow water... then he moved.

Step... pause... breathe.

He crossed the stream one inch at a time. The water splashed against his legs, feeling like needles of ice, but Barnaby remained as still as a mountain. He was not thinking about the top of the High Peak. He was only thinking about the very next step.

By midnight, the forest was very dark and very quiet. The other animals had gone to sleep, thinking Barnaby was still miles behind. But Barnaby was now entering the Whispering Pine Woods. The wind began to blow, whistling through the needles—whoooo... whoooo. The trees seemed to lean in, their long shadows stretching out like fingers.

Are you tired, Barnaby? the wind whispered. The mountain is so high... and you are so small.

Barnaby felt the weight of the moon-sliver. It felt heavier now, as if it were soaking up the darkness around it. His muscles ached. His shell felt like it was made of lead. But he didn't look up at the mountain. He looked at the ground just in front of his nose. He smelled the scent of pine needles and damp earth. He listened to the steady beat of his own heart.

Thrum... thrum... thrum.

He found a rhythm in the pain. He found a song in the slowness. He realized that when you move very slowly, you see things that everyone else misses. He saw a family of beetles sleeping under a leaf. He saw the way the dew gathered on a spider’s web, looking like a string of pearls. He saw that the world was full of small wonders, and he felt grateful to be awake to see them.

As the sky began to turn a soft, dusty purple... the color of a grape... Barnaby reached the base of the High Peak. It was a wall of stone, steep and jagged. His breath came in short, misty puffs in the cold morning air.

I am almost there, he whispered to the sliver on his back.

The sliver was very dim now. It was almost the color of wood ash. Barnaby began to climb. This was the greatest challenge of all. He had to wedge his claws into the cracks of the rock. He had to pull his heavy body up, bit by bit. Sometimes, he would slip back an inch, and the sound of his shell scraping against the stone echoed through the silent woods. Scritch... scratch.

He did not panic. He did not rush. When he slipped, he simply waited until his heart was quiet again, and then he moved forward.

Step... pause... breathe.

Finally, just as the very first ray of the golden sun began to peek over the horizon, Barnaby reached the very tip of the High Peak. He was exhausted. His legs were shaking, and his head felt heavy. But he stood at the edge of the world, where the earth touched the sky.

Above him, the crescent moon was waiting, looking thin and pale in the morning light. Barnaby took a deep breath. He tilted his shell with a slow, careful grace. The moon-sliver began to slide. It moved slowly at first, then picked up speed, glowing brighter and brighter as it neared the edge.

With a final, shimmering leap, the sliver flew into the air. It didn't fall. It rose. It zipped upward like a silver bird, trailing a ribbon of stardust behind it. It clicked back into place on the moon with a sound that felt like a bell ringing inside a dream.

Ding.

Suddenly, the moon flared with a brilliant, silver light. A wave of cool, peaceful energy washed down from the sky, covering the Silver Sedge Marsh, the Whispering Pines, and the High Peak. The light touched Barnaby, warming his tired limbs and soothing his aching shell.

Barnaby looked up and saw the moon whole again. It seemed to wink at him.

The turtle closed his golden eyes. He had no more steps to take. He tucked his head inside his shell, feeling the safety of his home. He smelled the clean, cold air of the mountain top. He listened to the silence of the morning.

He had been slow, but he had been enough. And as the sun rose to start the day, the slowest creature in the world fell into the deepest, sweetest sleep he had ever known.
